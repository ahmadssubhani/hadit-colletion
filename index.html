Knowledge guide

How hadith evidence is described

A plain-language guide to report types, narrator categories, and the scholars whose judgments Relegious records. Sunni and Shia vocabularies are shown separately where they differ.

Kinds of hadithNarrator categoriesAuthorities coveredHow judgment works
Part 1

“Kind of hadith” can mean four different things

These labels answer different questions. One report may be ahad by route count, marfuʿ by attribution, muttasil by continuity, and sahih by acceptance.

Acceptance · Sunni terminology

How strong is the evidence?

A judgment, not a topic
Sahih · sound

A connected chain of upright, accurate transmitters, free from hidden defect and contradiction with stronger evidence.

Hasan · good

Broadly acceptable, usually with narrator precision below the sahih level.

Daʿif · weak

One or more acceptance conditions are missing. Weakness has many causes and degrees.

Mawduʿ · fabricated

Judged invented and falsely attributed; more severe than ordinary weakness.

Acceptance · Twelver Shia terminology

How is the chain characterized?

Later fourfold system
Sahih · sound

An uninterrupted Imami chain whose transmitters are individually deemed reliable.

Hasan · good

An Imami chain containing a praised transmitter without an explicit reliability statement.

Muwaththaq · reliable

Transmitters are reliable, though one or more is not identified as Imami.

Daʿif · weak

Does not satisfy the stated chain conditions of the other classes.

Qawi · strong

A supplementary term used by some; definitions vary, so the scholar and method must be named.

Number of routes

How widely is it transmitted?

Mutawatir

Mass-transmitted through enough independent routes to preclude collusion under the applied method.

Ahad

Does not reach mutawatir; this does not automatically mean weak.

Mashhur

Usually three or more routes at each relevant level, short of mutawatir.

ʿAziz

Usually no fewer than two transmitters at each relevant level.

Gharib

At some point only one transmitter narrates the report.

Attribution

To whom is it attributed?

Qudsi

The Prophet relates meaning attributed to God, distinct from the Qur’an.

Marfuʿ

Attributed to the Prophet in word, action, approval, or description.

Mawquf

Attributed to a Companion rather than the Prophet.

Maqtuʿ

Attributed to a Successor or someone after that generation.

Chain continuity

Is the path connected?

Muttasil

Every transmitter received it from the person named before them.

Musnad

Often a connected report traced to the Prophet; usage varies.

Mursal

A Successor reports directly from the Prophet, omitting a Companion.

Munqatiʿ

A chain with a missing link not covered by a more specific label.

Muʿdal

Two or more consecutive transmitters are missing.

Muʿallaq

One or more transmitters are omitted from the compiler’s end of the chain.

Part 2

Categories and descriptions of narrators

Generation labels locate a narrator historically. Reliability terms record a named expert’s judgment.

Generation

Where does the narrator sit?

Sahabi · Companion

Under the common Sunni definition, met the Prophet as a believer and died Muslim.

Tabiʿi · Successor

Met one or more Companions, but not the Prophet.

Atbaʿ al-tabiʿin

The generation who learned from the Successors.

Companion of an Imam

Shia rijal works may organize a transmitter by the Imam from whom they narrated.

Compiler

The scholar recording the report in a book, not necessarily an originator of its chain.

Rijal assessment

How is the narrator described?

Always name the assessor
Thiqah

Trustworthy: a strong positive reliability judgment.

Thabt / mutqin

Firm or precise in retention and transmission.

Saduq

Truthful and generally acceptable, sometimes less precise than thiqah.

Maqbul

Acceptable under stated conditions; force varies by critic.

Majhul

Identity or reliability is insufficiently known.

Daʿif

Weak; the critic’s reason should be recorded.

Matruk

Abandoned: a severe judgment that reports are left aside.

Kadhdhab

Accused liar: an extremely severe criticism.

Part 4

Who “approves” or “rejects” a report?

1. Narrator criticism

Experts assess identity, integrity, precision, teachers, students, dates, and reported defects.

2. Chain analysis

Scholars test continuity, possible meetings, supporting routes, contradictions, and hidden defects.

3. Report judgment

A hadith critic applies a stated method to chain and text. Another qualified scholar may disagree.

Platform rule: Never say “approved by Islam.” Say “graded sahih by…”, “classed muwaththaq by…”, or “narrator declared thiqah by…”, with the exact work and citation. Numerical scores remain secondary editorial mappings.
